## Views and overlays

**Sky view.** The Sky switch (or `V`) shows the sky as seen from the place at the center of the Earth view, at the time of the time scrubber, which is always shown in this view. Drag (one finger on touch) to look around, scroll or pinch to zoom (`+` / `-`, the zoom buttons; double-click zooms in on a point, shift + double-click out), arrow keys turn the view, `R` or ⟲ looks south again, and `,` `.` `<` `>` `T` step and play the time as on the Earth. The ground hides what is below the horizon, the horizon carries the compass points, and a right ascension / declination grid (finer as you zoom in; the celestial equator brighter) is drawn over the sky. The HUD shows the RA and Dec under the cursor, the azimuth and altitude of the view center and the field of view. The URL keeps the observer and time and adds `&view=sky&look=<azimuth>,<altitude>,<field>` in degrees. The map controls (layers, search, projections, measuring, the terminator) are hidden here. Stars come from the HYG catalog (see [data](data.md)). Constellation figures and names (Stellarium's `western` sky culture: the 88 IAU constellations with their traditional figures, secondary lines fainter) are on by default; ✦ or `C` hides them, and the URL then carries `&figures=off`. Lines stop short of their stars, and names that would sit on a star's name move just below or above it, or are left out. The Sun, the Moon and the planets are placed for the observer and time: the Sun and Moon as disks at their true size (at least 6 px), the Moon with its phase and lit side facing the Sun and with parallax (up to a degree, from where you stand), the planets as colored dots sized by a typical magnitude (Uranus and Neptune only when the view goes faint enough). The Moon uses the main terms of Meeus's lunar theory (within 0.013° of JPL Horizons from 1900 to 2100), the planets JPL's Keplerian elements. The Milky Way glows behind the stars at night (see [data](data.md)). The telescope button (or `D`, and `&survey=dss` in the URL) shows the Digitized Sky Survey's color photographs of the whole sky instead: a 0.8 MB atlas for wide views, then tiles streamed as you zoom in (nebulae such as M42 resolve at a few degrees). It is off by default because the images come from third-party servers (the ESA ESASky and NASA/IPAC IRSA mirrors of CDS's HiPS), which then see your requests. When the Sun is up the sky turns blue and the stars fade through twilight (from 12° below the horizon to 6° above), leaving the Moon and the brightest planets.

**Languages.** Languages in the overlays menu (the layers button in the control column, or `W`) adds the world's languages from Glottolog (see [data](data.md)) as dots at each language's location, colored by family (the twelve largest families have their own colors, the rest are grey, isolates white; the legend lists them), with names once zoomed in below about 2,500 km. The menu in the legend colors the dots instead by endangerment (Glottolog's status, from not endangered to extinct) or by one of 15 grammar features from Grambank (definite articles, "he" and "she", gender by sex, numeral classifiers, inclusive and exclusive "we", case, ergativity, verb-final and verb-initial order, numeral and noun order, prepositions, postpositions, evidentials, polite "you", base-20 numerals); languages without data for the choice are hidden, and the legend says how many. Hovering a dot shows the language, its family and macroarea, and the value shown, and the search box finds languages by name once the layer has loaded. The URL adds `&languages=1`, or the coloring's id (`&languages=aes`, `&languages=GB057`). A dot marks one point for a language, not the area where it is spoken; an extinct language sits where it was last spoken.

**Societies.** Societies in the overlays menu (or `K`) shows the 1,291 societies of the Ethnographic Atlas (from D-PLACE, see [data](data.md)) as dots where each was studied, colored by one coded practice that the menu in the legend picks: subsistence (the dominant activity), descent, marital residence, settlement pattern, political levels above the local community, or class differentiation. The legend counts the societies with each value. Hovering a dot shows the society, its region and focal year, and its value. Each society is coded for one time (mostly the 19th and 20th centuries), not for today. The languages and societies layers share the legend, so turning one on turns the other off. The URL adds `&societies=<variable>` (for example `EA042`).

**Deep time.** Deep time in the overlays menu (or `A`) moves today's continents back in time with a plate tectonic model (Merdith et al. 2021, see [data](data.md)), from the present to 1000 million years ago: the age panel's slider and buttons, or `,` `.` (a million years) and `<` `>` (ten), pick the age, shown with its geological period. Land is colored by where it is today (North America, South America, Europe, northern Asia, Africa, India, the rest of Asia, Australia and Antarctica), so Pangaea and Gondwana read at a glance. The shapes are today's coastlines carried by their plates, not the shorelines of the time (sea level and lost land are not modeled), and before about 320 million years ago the model's longitudes are not constrained. Modern places, borders and the other overlays are hidden. The URL adds `&ma=<age>`.

**Historical borders.** Historical borders in the overlays menu (or `H`) shows the world's polities at a year from 3400 BCE to 2024, from Cliopatria (see [data](data.md)), in place of today's countries: their fills, borders and names change with the year panel's slider, its buttons, or `,` `.` (a year) and `<` `>` (a century). The coastlines, lakes and ice stay today's. Hovering shows the polity under the cursor with the years its borders held. The layer picker, the day/night terminator and the country panel are hidden meanwhile. The URL adds `&year=<year>`, negative for BCE (there is no year 0). Borders, names and dates of past polities are often uncertain; Cliopatria is one version of them. Lang in the year panel (or `J`) colors the polities by the language family of their main languages instead, from the Seshat Global History Databank, with a legend; hovering then also names the languages ("Roman Empire (117 to 126) · Latin (Indo-European)"), and the URL adds `&langfam=1`. Seshat codes the languages of a polity's government and elite, not every language spoken in it, and only some polities: most of the ancient world's area, about half from 1000 to 1800, and almost none after 1900 (shown dark grey, "No data in Seshat").

**Solar system view.** The Solar system switch (or `V` again) shows the Sun, the planets and the Moon in space at the time of the time scrubber, with the stars and the Milky Way of the sky view behind them. The camera orbits a body: drag (one finger) to orbit it, scroll or pinch (`+` / `-`, the zoom buttons) to move in or out (from just above the surface to 500 au), arrow keys orbit in steps. Tap or click a body or its name, pick one in the menu under the switch, or press `0`–`9` to fly there; `R` or ⟲ flies back to the Sun from above the inner planets. Bodies are drawn to scale, lit by the Sun, the Earth, Moon, Mars, Mercury and Jupiter with their real surfaces (NASA and USGS maps, see [data](data.md); loaded as a body grows on screen, and the HUD shows the longitude and latitude under the cursor), the others in their color (Saturn with faint bands, Saturn with its rings and the planet's shadow on them), and as colored dots when they are too small to see; each planet's orbit is drawn through it (from its current orbital elements, hidden where it passes behind a body), and the Moon's path around the Earth over the last month. Positions are those of the sky view (JPL's Keplerian elements; the Earth and the Moon placed about their barycenter with the lunar theory). The HUD shows the body or the RA and Dec under the cursor, the body orbited and the distance from its surface. Spacecraft (green diamonds, with their trajectory over the data's window), dwarf planets and asteroids (grey dots, faint orbits) and comets (blue, with a short tail pointing away from the Sun, and their orbits, hyperbolic ones out to 60 au) come from NASA JPL and are refreshed weekly (see [deployment](deployment.md#spacecraft-and-small-bodies-weekly-refresh)); hovering one shows what it is, its distance from the Sun and the date of the data. Spacecraft appear only from a month before that date to a year after; small bodies are propagated from their orbital elements for any time. The satellite button (or `O`; `&objects=off` in the URL) hides them. Play runs the time on without wrapping at the end of the year. The URL adds `&view=solar&orbit=<body>,<azimuth>,<altitude>,<distance in au>`.

## Countries, measuring and units

Hovering a country (desktop) highlights it and shows its name in the HUD. Clicking or tapping a country opens a panel with its flag, name, continent, region, population and ISO codes (on a thematic layer, the layer's value comes first); clicking the ocean, the × button or `Esc` closes it. The panel opens about a third of a second after the click, once it is clear the click is not the start of a double-click (fly-in); measure points are placed immediately. Keyboard cycling (`Tab`, `[`, `]`; in measure mode these select measure points instead) visits the countries whose representative point (inside the largest piece of the country) is on screen: in front of the horizon on the globe, inside the outline on a flat map. They come in reading order: top to bottom in bands of a sixth of the viewport height, left to right inside a band. Cycling highlights the country and opens or updates the info panel, whose live region makes screen readers announce the name; the view never moves, because every candidate is already on screen. `src/country_cycle.rs` holds the pure ordering and stepping logic. Hover and click are ignored until the country data has loaded, which happens after the first frame. Hover and click are forgiving: when the pointer is inside no country, the nearest country whose outline passes within 8 px (16 px for touch and pen) wins, so tiny islands such as Malta, Jersey or Pacific atolls can be hit without zooming in. The radius is in screen pixels, so it does not change with zoom; a country that contains the point always wins, open ocean still hits nothing, and only outlines that are visible (not behind the globe, inside a flat map's domain) count. `I` uses the same 8 px slack at the view center. `Countries::hit_near` prefilters by an expanded lon/lat box and measures point-to-segment distances in screen space for the few segments that survive (about 0.01 ms per query in release builds).

Measure points can be edited. A press within 11 px of a point (22 px for touch and pen) grabs it instead of panning; it selects the point (blue halo; a faint white halo marks hover), and dragging moves it with the path, legs and total updating live. A second click or tap on the same point within the double-click interval removes it. To place a new point close to an existing one, click or tap that point once to select it, then click or tap again on the outer half of its hit area (farther than 5.5 px from its center for a mouse, 11 px for touch) after the double-click interval: that adds a point where you pressed instead of grabbing the old one. `Shift`+click adds a point anywhere, even right on top of another one. Pressing near a point's center never adds, so double-clicking a point still removes it. Dragging never adds. If you pick a point by dragging it, it stays selected, so the next click beside it adds. Removing a point merges the two neighbouring legs into one; double-click does not fly in while measuring. `Backspace` removes the selected point, or the last one when none is selected. `Tab` steps through points and lets focus move on after the last (`Shift`+`Tab` goes backwards from the first), `[` and `]` wrap around. Only points that are visible can be grabbed: not behind the globe, and not outside a flat map's outline. The hit test and drag use `Camera::project` and `Camera::pick`, so they work in every projection, including across the side edge of a flat map.

Measure distances are WGS84 geodesic lengths (the shortest path on the ellipsoid), not great-circle lengths on a sphere, so they differ from a spherical estimate by up to about 0.5% and match tools such as GeographicLib to under a millimetre (for example JFK to Heathrow is 5,555 km, and a quarter of the meridian is 10,001.966 km). The drawn path is still a great circle on the globe; only the numbers use the ellipsoid. `src/geodesic.rs` solves the inverse problem: Vincenty's iteration for most pairs, and Karney's azimuth-bisection method with numerically integrated auxiliary-sphere integrals for pairs more than 150° apart, where Vincenty can fail to converge. Meridians, poles, the ±180° seam and exactly antipodal points are handled directly. The scale bar and altitude readout stay spherical (radius 6371 km), because they convert screen pixels at the view center and the difference is far below what a pixel can show.

Units cycle km → mi → nmi and apply to the scale bar, the altitude readout and the measure panel. The choice is saved in the browser's `localStorage` and restored on reload. It is deliberately not part of the URL hash, so a shared link does not impose the sharer's preference; if storage is unavailable (private mode) the unit simply resets to km. The scale bar shows a round 1/2/5 × 10ⁿ distance in the chosen unit, and below one km, mi or nmi it switches to m, ft or m (a nautical mile is defined as exactly 1852 m, so metres are the natural sub-mile unit at sea). The HUD shows a scale bar (a round 1/2/5 × 10ⁿ distance) for the ground distance at the screen center. It is hidden when you are zoomed out far enough (above about 1 globe radius of altitude) that the scale varies too much across the view.

Rotation has two modes. **North-up** (the default) keeps north at the top and pans like a flat map; dragging over a pole tilts through it, after which north points down and the compass shows 180°. Near a pole the view swings quickly as you drag, as meridians converge there, and a sideways drag of the pole itself cannot follow the cursor exactly. **Free** is trackball-style: the surface always follows the cursor and the view never rolls on its own. Turning the view (alt-drag, twist, `Q`/`E`, `F`) switches to free; the compass button turns blue while free. Clicking the compass, `F`, or reset (`R`, ⟲) smoothly turns back to north and locks north-up.

## Links, time and the terminator

The current view is saved in the URL hash as `#lat,lon,altitude_km`, so you can link straight to a place, e.g. `#46.8,8.2,600`. A fourth field adds the bearing, the compass direction at the top of the screen in degrees, e.g. `#46.8,8.2,600,135`; it is written only when the view is not facing north, so older links keep working. A nonzero bearing opens in free mode. You can center on a pole exactly, e.g. `#90,0,6000` (longitude then picks which meridian runs down the screen in north-up). A non-default layer is added as `&layer=<id>` (`political`, `mono`, `imagery`, `terrain`, `night`, `pop`, `gdp` or `income`), e.g. `#46.8,8.2,600&layer=mono`; opening an `imagery`, `terrain` or `night` link downloads that image first and shows the political layer if that fails. Switching layers never moves the camera. A flat projection is added as `&proj=<id>` (`equirect`, `mercator`, `robinson` or `mollweide`; the globe writes nothing, so old links still open the globe), e.g. `#47,8.5,1500&proj=mercator`. On a flat map the center is the point under the middle of the screen: its longitude is the central meridian, and there is never a bearing. A Mercator link centered past about 85° is pulled back onto the map.

The day/night terminator is off by default. `&sun=on` turns it on at load, and `&t=<unix seconds>` pins the sun to a fixed time instead of the clock (the visual tests use this so screenshots are deterministic). Both are read at load and again when you edit the hash by hand (a missing `sun` means off). The app writes them back: while the terminator is on, the hash and the share link include `&sun=on`, and `&t=` only if the time is pinned. In live-clock mode `t` is never written, so the hash does not change every minute. Turning the terminator off removes both from the hash, but a pinned time is remembered in the page and returns if you turn it back on. While on, the sun follows the clock and is refreshed once a minute by a timer. That timer is the only work the app does while idle, and it only redraws when the terminator is on.

**Time scrubber.** While the terminator is on, a panel at the bottom left (above the HUD, which moves up to make room) has a date slider (day of the year, 1 to 365 or 366), a UTC time-of-day slider (one-minute steps) with the browser's local time beside it, a play button, a Day / Year switch and a Now button. Moving either slider pins the sun to that time, which is the same `&t=` pin as above, so the URL and share link follow it (written after the slider has been still for a quarter of a second, not on every input event). The date slider keeps the time of day and the time slider keeps the date. Now removes the pin and goes back to the clock. Play pins the time first, then advances it every frame: in Day mode one simulated day takes 10 s, in Year mode the time of day stays fixed and the date runs through one year in 12 s, wrapping December to January of the same year. Any manual change pauses play. While playing the globe counts as moving for dynamic resolution and the URL waits until you pause; paused or live, nothing runs per frame (live mode still refreshes once a minute). With `prefers-reduced-motion`, play still works but moves in coarse jumps instead of continuously: one hour (Day) or one week (Year) every 0.7 s. It works on every projection, since `globe.frag` draws the terminator on all of them. The scrubber's range is 1970 to 2099 and the time math is in `src/scrub.rs` (UTC only, so no daylight saving).

## Flat maps

Choosing a flat projection morphs the globe into it (about 0.9 s); with `prefers-reduced-motion` the change is immediate. The camera keeps one pose for both: longitude and latitude of the center, and altitude as the zoom. On a flat map the view is a plain 2D one: drag pans (sideways it turns the central meridian, so longitude wraps; vertically it stops at the top and bottom edge), scroll and pinch zoom toward the cursor, and the bearing is locked to north. Cylindrical maps (equirectangular, Mercator) repeat sideways when you zoom out past one world; Robinson and Mollweide show a single world inside their outline. Mercator ends at ±85.0511°, where it is square, and shows nothing beyond. Altitude on a flat map is just the zoom: at the same altitude a flat map is drawn a third as large as the globe, so zooming all the way out fits the world on screen. The scale bar shows the east-west ground distance at the center row.

Cursor readout, country hover and click, labels, search flights and the measure tool all go through `Camera::pick` / `Camera::project`, which switch on the projection, so they behave the same on every map. Measure paths are great circles sampled and projected, and break where they cross the side edge. Labels and the measure path are hidden while the morph runs.
